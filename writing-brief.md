# Executive case-study writing brief

This guide distils the structure and editorial choices of the five supplied samples, including the Product Genius example. It contains no client facts, sample metrics, company claims or reusable sample passages. Only current confirmed permitted intake evidence supplies facts. Apply the guide even when the reference library is empty.

## Audience and voice
Write for Fortune 500 C-suite executives evaluating an operational change. Use plain, specific business language: confident, measured, crisp and direct. British English. Describe the anonymous client in third person and the intervention in our partnership voice: "We helped...", "We redesigned..." or "The organisation partnered with us..." where delivery is evidenced. Never invent a provider name or a partnership claim not supported by the intake. Do not refer to "the delivery team" in ordinary prose. Do not mistake the solution for the outcome: the solution changes the workflow; the outcome is the supported change for the client.

Natural medium-length sentences and short developed paragraphs, normally two or three connected sentences, rather than choppy fragments or dense text blocks. Short linking paragraphs are appropriate; never expand a sentence just to meet a paragraph quota. Do not repeatedly use the same closing formula across stories. Avoid superlatives, generic transformation claims, dramatic adjectives and executive buzzwords.

## Editorial selection before writing
Identify a single business tension supported by the evidence: the operating demand, the process constraint and its consequence. Select the intervention that addresses it and the strongest permitted result. Organise the complete story around that change. Extraction completeness does not mean publication completeness: leave out information that does not advance the story.

Do not use every fact. Revenue, employee count, exhaustive sector lists, integrations and deployment dates are optional context, not obligatory content. Never turn product-catalog revenue into product revenue. Never turn users in a result calculation into the size of a sales organisation. Do not describe a client as global, large or Fortune 500 merely because the audience is Fortune 500.

Describe capabilities through what people do: enter requirements, assess relevant options, inspect evidence, confirm a decision and use the output, but only when each action is supported. Explain Cognitive Decisioning or reasoning through its role in that process, not a feature inventory. Technical systems belong in the story only when they explain the constraint or intervention. A stack of database/cloud product names is not a business explanation.

## Case Study sections and purpose
Keep this exact internal order: Title; Subtext; Context; Key Metrics; The Challenge; The Solution; The Outcome; CTA.

Title: a concrete supported improvement, often "How We Helped [anonymous audience] [operational result]" or "How [permitted solution] Helped [anonymous client type] [result]". Prefer roughly 10–18 words. A confirmed numeric result is welcome when it makes the benefit clearer; nonnumeric is appropriate when a result needs lengthy qualification. Do not imply a different metric, round values, invent faster decision-making or copy a source sentence as a headline.

Subtext: one concise sentence connecting the intervention and main supported result. Avoid capability lists, vague "dramatically", and context biography. Keep essential qualifiers. A headline result may recur here only to explain the intervention.

Context: anonymous client activity and operational scale only as relevant, then the business situation. Briefly establish why finding information or making a decision matters in this workflow. A short "We..." intervention sentence can orient the story. Do not restate Subtext. No exhaustive products, industries, revenue figures or user statistics merely because the intake supplies them.

Key Metrics: two or three selective compact bullets when supported; one is enough. Label realised savings, recovered capacity, estimates and ROI accurately. Do not copy a whole results table or put all figures here. Preserve complete metric details; do not round to K/M merely because an example does.

The Challenge: explain the previous operating process, why it breaks down and the supported consequence for throughput, dependency, cost or response. Connect the sentences rather than list intake answers. Normally 2–3 compact paragraphs. Use a small baseline list only if it makes the constraint clearer. No architecture tutorial, duplicated arithmetic or inventories of symptoms. Do not convert an intended improvement into an achieved result.

The Solution: normally the longest section, 3–5 compact paragraphs where evidence permits. Open with our intervention and its scope. Follow the user's changed workflow in order: starting input; how relevant information/options are found or evaluated; what output and supporting evidence are presented; what the person reviews/decides; what happens next. Include a structured summary or email only if the implementation evidence confirms it, not merely because it was desired in the before-state. Explain how this addresses the Challenge. A reduced dependency is not eliminated human involvement. Do not add speed, certificates, integration behaviour or decision authority absent from the intake.

The Outcome: select the effects not already explained, then state the evidenced operating capability the client gained. Compact result bullets followed by a short paragraph are appropriate. Do not repeat the entire opening scorecard. Do not restate features. Do not invent staff redeployment, higher-value work, customer engagement, revenue causality, competitive advantage or scale. Capacity equivalents are not jobs removed, and opportunities are not realised revenue. A modest supported conclusion is stronger than unsupported strategic language.

CTA: use the approved pain-point invitation template supplied by the app. For Product Genius, ask about manual product searches, connect the invitation to evidence-backed recommendations, and close with a short action line. Other workflows use the neutral approved invitation. Do not add numeric promises, guarantees or new product capabilities.

## Grounding and presentation
Aim around 380–550 words for a rich Case Study, without padding; the examples vary. Paragraphs are separated by actual blank lines. Use ordinary bullets for metrics and plain text for prose. No source IDs or editorial notes in the reader-facing draft: fact_ids belong in section metadata. Every factual section, including headline/subtext, needs supporting IDs for every material claim. Keep human approval separate from automated checks.

Exact metric value, unit, qualifier, measurement period and comparison must be retained. When a numeric headline is used, keep the exact value/unit and important qualification in the headline and retain its complete period/comparison in a linked results section. No computed ratios, rounded savings or verbal fractions without evidence. Estimated/calculated results must remain labelled. Reference material supplies style only, never claims, product behaviour, client identities or numbers.

## One-Pager (provisional)
Order: Headline; Client and Context; Challenge; Solution; Impact or Key Results; Why It Matters; CTA. Same confirmed facts, approved output-neutral plan and publication rules. Aim 220–320 words with sufficiently rich evidence. Select the same business tension and outcome; write a compact independent narrative rather than truncate the Case Study or copy every section. Use developed economical paragraphs, selective metrics and grounded practical relevance. Why It Matters adds supported meaning rather than repeats the Impact bullets. The five examples do not establish an approved One-Pager template.


## Mandatory opening and readability pattern
Title → Subtext → Context → Key Metrics → Challenge → Solution → Outcome → pain-point CTA. Context is not a company biography: anonymous client and relevant demand, the operating problem in 1–2 sentences, then a separate short "We..." change paragraph. Metrics follow immediately as a selective scorecard. Do not give every section a list of intake facts.

The Solution starts by establishing the partnership and naming the evidenced service, then follows how it helps the user complete the work. Use short, connected paragraphs with one idea each. Do not bury decisions in long technical sentences. No obligatory deployment timeline or list of integrations. Outcome adds distinct results and closes with a modest supported operating capability. Avoid reusing the full opening scorecard.

Quantity fields have identities: employee range, catalogue size, monthly requests, adoption, time, money and capacity must never substitute for each other. Preserve metric scope and exact values. A before-state description belongs in narrative context, not pasted after every bullet. Baseline/comparison metadata is concise; the full original description remains available as source evidence.
