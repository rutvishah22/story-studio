# Case Study Format and Style Guide

Derived from five supplied case-study examples. This guide contains structural and editorial observations only. It excludes their company identities, product names, client details, actual metrics, and distinctive passages. The source PDF has not been added to Story Studio or sent to its inference provider. This sanitised guide is applied to drafting and writing review in the app.

## 1. Observed section sequence

All five examples follow this underlying order:

1. Title
2. Subtext
3. Context and brief transformation summary
4. Highlighted metrics or a baseline snapshot
5. The Challenge
6. The Solution
7. The Outcome
8. CTA

Only “The Challenge,” “The Solution,” and “The Outcome” consistently appear as named body headings. “Subtext:” appears as an explicit label. Context and the opening metrics are generally unlabelled. The CTA appears as a bold question, explanatory copy, and a short bold action line rather than under a heading called “CTA.”

This agrees with the PRD's eight-part Case Study structure. Keep those eight internal sections for drafting and validation, while allowing a reader-facing rendition to omit the Context, Key Metrics and CTA labels.

The PDF includes separator/cover pages and a blank page. Those are compilation artifacts, not case-study sections. Each substantive example occupies two pages in this document. That is an observed layout, not a required length or final design specification.

## 2. Section-by-section writing pattern

| Section | Observed purpose and presentation | Reusable drafting rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title | A bold, result-led headline, usually beginning with “How.” It identifies an organisation type or the delivery team and a concrete improvement. | State one supported central outcome. Use an exact result or before/after comparison only when confirmed. A descriptive workflow headline is acceptable when no verified metric exists. |
| Subtext | One or two sentences summarising the intervention and principal benefit. Often introduces the solution and broader service proposition. | Explain what changed and the supported benefit. Use approved solution names only if present in the permitted intake or approved messaging. Do not inherit names or capabilities from examples. |
| Context | A short paragraph about the client's sector, scale, activity and operational situation, followed by a brief transformation summary. | Help the reader understand who faced the problem and which workflow changed. Use permitted identity details; do not invent scale or geography. |
| Key Metrics | Compact bullets, standalone bold metric lines, or a two-column label/result table. Commonly two or three opening items. One example uses baseline conditions instead of results. | Prefer confirmed results for the PRD's Key Metrics section. If baseline evidence is needed, label it clearly as “Before” or put it in The Challenge. Never mix baseline and outcome figures without labels. |
| The Challenge | Describes the previous workflow, manual steps, dependencies, bottlenecks and business consequence. Some examples add a small baseline list/table. | Explain what people previously did, why it constrained work, and the supported cost or consequence. Concrete operating details should carry the argument. |
| The Solution | Usually the longest section. Introduces the intervention, then walks through the revised workflow in order. Explains technical capabilities through what users can do. Several examples explain evidence, reasoning, review or escalation. | Describe input → processing/change → output → human use/review. Mention oversight or evidence mechanisms only when verified for this story. End with what changed in people's work. |
| The Outcome | Presents supported results as bullets or prose, then explains their practical meaning. Some examples separate cost, capacity and speed; one is especially concise. | Report measured results with their qualifiers and periods. Distinguish actual savings, estimated value, opportunity and recovered capacity. Explain a broader implication only when evidence supports it. |
| CTA | A question tied to the reader's analogous operational problem, followed by one benefit-focused invitation and a short action line. | Use problem question → approved invitation → approved action text. CTA language requires approved messaging; the examples do not authorize product promises. |

## 3. Tone and voice

- Professional, confident and business-oriented, with an explanatory rather than conversational voice.
- Plain operational language: describe tasks, people, constraints and results before technical architecture.
- Mostly third-person client narration, with occasional first-person plural statements about the delivery team's intervention. This alternation is observed; choose a consistent approved house convention for future drafts.
- Mostly past tense for the previous state, implementation and achieved outcomes. CTA copy uses present tense and addresses the reader directly.
- Natural medium-length sentences that connect the operational detail to its business meaning. Avoid choppy fragments or chains of very short sentences. Paragraphs usually develop one point in two or three sentences.
- Active verbs and named actors make the workflow easy to follow.
- Predominantly British English spelling, with some inconsistency. Recommended house rule: use British English consistently unless a supplied proper name requires otherwise.
- The main prose is explanatory; the headline, highlighted results and CTA carry more promotional emphasis.

## 4. How the narrative is built

The examples repeatedly use this reasoning sequence:

**Operational situation → manual constraint → workflow change → user control → supported result → business meaning.**

The solution is presented as a change to the working process, rather than a disconnected feature list. Technical terms are usually followed by an explanation of the task they support. Several examples contrast the old process with the new one to make the improvement concrete.

Three examples close the Outcome by distinguishing the immediate improvement from a broader operational benefit. Reuse that reasoning pattern where supported, but vary the sentence construction rather than copying the recurring wording.

Human review, transparent reasoning and specialist involvement recur in the examples. Treat these as story-specific facts to verify, not universal promises about every implementation.

## 5. Presentation conventions

- Single-column, text-led pages on a white background.
- Large bold title, bold body headings and generous spacing between sections.
- Left-aligned paragraphs with restrained formatting.
- Selective bold emphasis for important metrics, solution terms and a few key concepts.
- Bullets for small sets of results; simple label/value rows or tables for comparisons and baseline information.
- No recurring testimonial, customer quote, chart, image or logo module in these five examples. Those should not become required sections based on this sample.
- CTA question receives heading-like emphasis. The final action line is short and bold.
- Opening results sometimes repeat later in the narrative. Repeat a result only when it serves a different purpose, and preserve its exact meaning each time.

## 6. Suggested length controls

These are practical starting targets inferred from the examples, not a fixed template approved by the company:

The substantive examples contain approximately 380–617 words each, including headings, metrics and CTA copy. Four are approximately 380–427 words; one is longer at approximately 617. Counts use whitespace-normalised PDF text and are approximate.

| Section | Suggested target |
| --- | --- |
| Title | About 12–20 words |
| Subtext | About 25–45 words |
| Context and transformation summary | About 50–85 words |
| Key Metrics | Usually 2–3 concise items, if evidence exists |
| The Challenge | About 90–180 words |
| The Solution | About 140–250 words |
| The Outcome | About 50–150 words; bullets where useful |
| CTA | About 30–55 words across the question, invitation and action |

Section ranges are flexible and should not all be filled to their upper limits. Evidence determines length. Never pad a short intake to meet a target. Final page count depends on visual design and is outside the drafting MVP.

## 7. What to standardise rather than copy

The examples are useful style evidence, but are not automatically a factual or editorial gold standard:

- Opening metric blocks vary between baseline and outcome information. Standardise their roles explicitly.
- Some results have estimates, approximations, opportunity language or capacity equivalents. Preserve those distinctions; an opportunity is not realised revenue, and recovered capacity is not necessarily a headcount reduction.
- There are variations in rounding, shorthand, multiplier notation and time descriptions. Preserve the new intake's confirmed metric details instead of copying these conventions indiscriminately.
- A few assertions about broader business value, system behaviour or continuing improvement would need supporting evidence in a new story.
- Product vocabulary and repeated brand positioning occur frequently. Only use separately approved terminology applicable to the current intake.
- Occasional tense or grammatical inconsistencies should be edited, not made part of the style profile.

## 8. Reusable AI instruction profile

Write a business case study using these internal sections in order: Title; Subtext; Context; Key Metrics; The Challenge; The Solution; The Outcome; CTA.

Use a professional, measured, business-first voice and consistent British English. Prefer active, naturally developed sentences and concise paragraphs with one main point. Write for business decision-makers evaluating a similar operational problem: simple, crisp, direct and specific. Avoid repetitive summaries, generic selling, and invented audience assumptions. Open with the strongest supported outcome. Establish the client's permitted context and prior workflow, explain the constraint, then describe the revised workflow in sequence. Translate technical capabilities into concrete user actions. Report confirmed outcomes and explain their supported operational meaning.

Use compact metrics bullets or simple label/result rows. Keep baseline conditions separate from achieved results. Preserve exact metric values, units, time periods, qualifiers and comparisons. Distinguish actual outcomes from estimates, opportunities and capacity equivalents. Use selective emphasis and avoid unnecessary jargon, hype and repeated stock phrases.

Only confirmed permitted facts from the current source record may supply claims. This writing profile supplies no client facts, product capabilities, metrics or approved CTA promises. If evidence is missing, omit the claim or expose the limitation. Use approved CTA messaging; otherwise retain a visible placeholder.
